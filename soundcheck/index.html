How it works

Five sliders. Five numbers. Then swap headphones and do it again.

Each slider plays one signal. You move it until you find the edge — the point where the tone vanishes, or where two tones sound equally loud — and press Mark. That number goes on the scoreboard under the headphones you named.

1

Name the pair

“HD 650 + Apple dongle”, “AirPods Pro 2”, whatever is on your head. The chain matters, so name the chain.

2

Slide to the edge, mark it

Fine-tune with − / + or the arrow keys. A landmark under the display tells you what the number means — “27.5 Hz: A0, the lowest key on a piano”.

3

Swap, repeat, read the board

Same signals, same ears, same volume. Whatever differs between the columns is the gear.

The five numbers

What you actually find out.

Not “warm” or “analytical”. Frequencies and decibels — the vocabulary the spec sheet uses, measured on your head instead of a dummy's.

Hz · lower is deeper

Bass extension

Slide a sine down from 300 Hz until it stops being a pitch. Well-extended cans still deliver a tone at 20–25 Hz; small drivers give up around 40.

Hz · higher is airier

Treble extension

Slide up from 3 kHz until it vanishes, then back until it returns. Yes, your age is in this number — but it is the same age for every pair you test.

± dB vs 1 kHz

Bass level

A 1 kHz reference alternates with 100 Hz. Match their loudness. Positive means the bass needed help; negative means the pair is bass-forward.

± dB vs 1 kHz

Treble level

Same match with 8 kHz. Negative is bright; positive is dark. Compare it to the bass number and you have the tilt in two figures.

Hz · where it bites

Harshness peak

Hand-sweep 2–12 kHz and mark the spot that jumps out. Around 6 kHz it is sibilance; near 3 kHz it is shout; above 9 it is tizz.

Why signals, not songs

A sine wave has no taste.

Headphones are usually judged with music, and music drags taste along with it. A studio monitor and a bass-tuned consumer set sound “wrong” to each other's fans, and a track you love will flatter whichever pair plays it the way you're used to.

A test signal doesn't care. Either the 24 Hz tone is there or it isn't. Either 100 Hz is louder than 1 kHz or it isn't. The same five signals go into every pair, and the only thing that changes between columns is the gear.

Everything is synthesized in the browser with the Web Audio API — sweeps, tones, pink noise, a kick drum, a Karplus–Strong plucked string — sample by sample, at whatever rate your DAC is running. No codec, no stream, no file.

Your ears are part of the chain. So keep them constant: same person, same device volume, different headphones — and the differences are the headphones.
